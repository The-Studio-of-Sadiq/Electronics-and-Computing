# Part VI — Embedded and Real-Time Systems

Parts IV and V built a processor and taught it to run programs. This part puts that processor inside a product — a water meter, a motor drive, a flight controller, an EV battery pack, an infusion pump — where it must sense and act on the physical world through pins and buses, meet deadlines set by physics, live within kilobytes of memory and microamps of current, and keep working unattended for a decade or more. The abstraction this part builds is the **task**: a piece of firmware with a period, a deadline and a worst-case execution time, which can be scheduled, analysed and proved to meet its timing. Like every abstraction in the book, it holds only inside limits — electrical limits at the pins, bit-timing limits on the buses, energy budgets from the battery, and timing limits that interrupts, shared resources and priority inversion can break. The four chapters move from the architecture of an embedded system, through its interfaces and its timing, to the engineering practice that ties them into a product that can be built, tested and maintained.

## By the end of this part you should understand

- How an embedded system is modelled and partitioned between peripherals and software, and how a microcontroller is chosen against peripheral, compute, memory, energy, security and supply requirements.
- How memory and energy budgets are written and met: linker sections, stack bounds, Flash endurance, power modes and the average-current model of battery life.
- How superloop, interrupt-driven and RTOS-based firmware differ in worst-case response, how a device gets from its reset vector to `main`, and how bootloaders install signed updates without ever leaving the device unbootable.
- The electrical behaviour of GPIO and the bit-level timing of UART, SPI, I²C and CAN, and the fundamentals of USB and Ethernet, with the numbers that the standards set.
- How converters and sensors are interfaced without losing accuracy, and how a failing link is diagnosed with an oscilloscope and logic analyser.
- How interrupt latency and task response times are bounded, how cyclic, rate-monotonic and EDF scheduling are analysed, and how response-time analysis proves schedulability.
- Why races, deadlock and priority inversion occur, and how critical sections, mutexes with priority inheritance, queues, watchdogs and fault handlers contain them.
- How firmware is layered, made low-power, debugged on the target and validated with hardware-in-the-loop testing.

## How this part connects to the rest of the book

This part rests on Part IV: the memory maps, interrupt controllers, timers and peripheral blocks of Chapter 17, the caches and buses of Chapter 16, and the Flash and SRAM of Chapter 18. It uses the C and C++ of Chapter 21 throughout, and the logic levels, synchronisers and state machines of Part III at every pin. The converter architectures of Chapter 10 and the sensor amplifiers of Chapter 8 sit behind its analog interfaces. Looking forward, the controllers of Part VII's power converters and motor drives (Chapters 29–32) are real-time embedded systems of exactly this kind, and Chapter 35 returns to partitioning, accelerators and real-time constraints across processors and FPGAs.

Among the sibling books, *Circuits, Signals and Communications* supplies the transmission-line theory behind bus termination ([Ch. 14](https://github.com/The-Studio-of-Sadiq/Circuits-Signals-and-Communications/blob/main/part-3-advanced-network-theory/14-transmission-lines.md)), sampling, quantization and jitter ([Ch. 19](https://github.com/The-Studio-of-Sadiq/Circuits-Signals-and-Communications/blob/main/part-4-signals-and-systems/19-sampling-and-reconstruction.md)), real-time DSP budgets ([Ch. 21](https://github.com/The-Studio-of-Sadiq/Circuits-Signals-and-Communications/blob/main/part-5-digital-signal-processing/21-dsp-fundamentals.md)), CRCs ([Ch. 28](https://github.com/The-Studio-of-Sadiq/Circuits-Signals-and-Communications/blob/main/part-6-communication-theory/28-information-theory-and-coding.md)) and the cost of loop delay in digital control ([Ch. 35](https://github.com/The-Studio-of-Sadiq/Circuits-Signals-and-Communications/blob/main/part-7-control-system-fundamentals/35-control-design-fundamentals.md)). *Electrical Energy and Machines* supplies battery ratings ([Ch. 16](https://github.com/The-Studio-of-Sadiq/Electrical-Energy-and-Machines/blob/main/part-4-power-generation/16-energy-storage.md)) and the drive-control algorithms that run on these processors ([Ch. 12](https://github.com/The-Studio-of-Sadiq/Electrical-Energy-and-Machines/blob/main/part-3-motor-drives/12-ac-motor-drives.md)).
