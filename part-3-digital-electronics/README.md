# Part III — Digital Electronics

Part II ended where analog circuits hand their results to logic: a comparator decides, an ADC delivers a code. This part builds the digital half of electronics from that boundary. A bit is an abstraction imposed on a voltage, valid only while the voltage stays outside the undefined band and the clock leaves time for every signal to settle. Inside those limits digital design offers exact repeatability, cascading without accumulated noise, and designs that can be specified completely and verified on every input. Outside them come the characteristic digital failures: a field read with the wrong sign or byte order, an overflow that turns full forward into full reverse, a glitch that clocks a register, a synchroniser that fails once a month. This part develops the representations, logic, building blocks and timing rules that make synchronous digital systems work at first power-up and keep working.

## By the end of this part you should understand

- How integers, signed values, fixed-point fractions and IEEE 754 floating-point numbers are represented in bits, what each costs in range, resolution and hardware, and how overflow is detected, saturated or exploited.
- How requirements become Boolean equations and truth tables, how De Morgan's laws, Karnaugh maps and minimisation algorithms reduce them to gates, and what logic levels and noise margins a real gate family guarantees.
- How adders, multiplexers, decoders, encoders, comparators, ALUs and code converters are organised, and how their structure trades logic for propagation delay.
- How latches, flip-flops, registers, counters and shift registers store state, how finite-state machines are encoded, and how setup, hold, clock-domain crossings and resets constrain them.
- How a complete digital system goes from specification through state diagrams to an implementation, how glitches and asynchronous inputs are handled, and how test benches verify it before hardware.

## How this part connects to the rest of the book

Part III stands on Parts I and II: the comparators and converters of Chapter 10 are the analog side of every digital input, and the MOSFET switch of Chapter 4 lies beneath every gate, whose transistor-level design is deferred to Chapter 33. Part IV assembles these blocks into processors, memories and programmable logic described in HDLs; Part V programs them, Chapter 21 returning to integer types, bitwise operators and fixed point in C; Part VI builds embedded and real-time systems on them; and Part VIII returns to logic at chip scale, with synthesis and timing analysis in Chapter 34.

Three sibling books supply theory used here without re-derivation. Boolean algebra and functional completeness, and the floating-point error model, come from *[The Mathematics Universe](https://github.com/The-Studio-of-Sadiq/engineering-mathematics)*, Ch. 29 and Ch. 22. Finite-word-length effects in digital filters, sampling and quantisation, and transmission-line behaviour of fast board traces come from *[Circuits, Signals and Communications](https://github.com/The-Studio-of-Sadiq/Circuits-Signals-and-Communications)*, Ch. 19, 21 and 14. The carrier physics of the transistors that switch underneath every gate comes from *[Engineering Physics: Top Down](https://github.com/The-Studio-of-Sadiq/Engineering-Physics)*, Ch. 6 and Ch. 18.
